Data supports almost every part of a modern business, from day-to-day decisions to new products and services. The more an organization depends on data, the more it matters that the data is accurate, accessible, secure and used responsibly. That is the job of data governance. This article is a short overview of why governance matters and what a program is made of. ## Understanding data governance

Data governance is the overall management of data availability, usability, integrity and security in an organization. It establishes the policies, procedures, standards and roles that make sure data is handled consistently and responsibly. It is the framework that day-to-day data management operates within.

Governance lets an organization manage data as an asset. It keeps data consistent and trustworthy, makes clear who may use it and for what, and protects sensitive information from breaches and misuse. The practical results are decisions based on data people trust, fewer compliance surprises, and less time spent reconciling conflicting numbers.

## Why data governance matters

### Improved data quality

Governed data is accurate, consistent and reliable, so organizations can trust the data they use for analytics and reporting.

### Enhanced data security

Knowing what data you hold, where it is and who can access it reduces the risk of breaches, unauthorized access and data loss.

### Better decision-making

When people trust the data, they spend less time questioning reports and more time acting on them.

### Increased operational efficiency

Clear ownership, shared definitions and fewer duplicate datasets reduce rework and the cost of managing data.

## Key components of data governance

A governance program usually brings together six areas:

- **Data quality management:** rules, regular measurement and fixes at the source that keep data accurate, complete, consistent and timely.
- **Data stewardship:** named people or teams who make sure policies are followed, monitor quality, and resolve or escalate data issues.
- **Data security:** role-based access controls, encryption, and regular security reviews and audits.
- **Data privacy:** consent management, data minimization, anonymization or pseudonymization, and handling individuals' requests to access or delete their data, in line with laws such as the GDPR and the CCPA (as amended by the CPRA).
- **Data lifecycle management:** archiving, retention and deletion rules, often formalized in a retention schedule, so data is kept only as long as it is needed.
- **Regulatory compliance:** turning the laws and industry rules that apply to the organization into policies, controls and evidence that can be shown to auditors and regulators. This reduces the risk of penalties and makes compliance easier to demonstrate when asked.

## Where to start

Most programs begin with a framework of policies and standards for the data domains that matter most to the business, named data stewards for those domains, and clear policies for quality, security, privacy and lifecycle. Tools such as data catalogs, data quality monitoring and access management come after the policies and roles are clear, so they support an agreed way of working. Training, and regular review of a small set of measures, keep the program working as the business changes.
