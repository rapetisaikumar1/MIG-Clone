A retention schedule tells an organization how long to keep each type of record and what to do with it afterwards. On paper, that sounds straightforward. In practice, many schedules sit in a document that few people read, while records are kept indefinitely "just in case" or disposed of inconsistently.

Closing the gap between the schedule and everyday practice is one of the most valuable things a records program can do.

## Why schedules fail in practice

- **They are hard to apply.** Staff have to interpret a long document while filing a record.
- **Records aren't classified when they're created.** Without a record series attached, nobody knows which retention rule applies.
- **Disposition isn't anyone's job.** Even when a retention period has ended, no process prompts a decision.
- **Access is too broad or too narrow.** Records are either open to everyone or locked away from the people who need them.

## Turning a schedule into a working system

Start by organizing records into series that reflect how work is actually done, such as permits, council minutes and contracts. Attach retention rules to each series so that the right rule applies automatically when a record is created. Define who can view, edit and approve disposition for each series, based on role. Then automate the routine steps: reminders when retention periods end, approval workflows for disposition, and a documented trail of every decision.
